Global Stablecoin Development Comparison: Regulation-to-Rails Convergence and Divergent Issuance Models

Executive Summary

Stablecoin development during the period is best understood as a comparison between two dominant structures: first, rapid buildout of regulated payment and settlement rails by banks, payment networks, and infrastructure providers; second, increasingly differentiated issuance and product models shaped by jurisdiction-specific regulation, collateral design, and yield constraints. Across the dataset, Europe and parts of Asia show repeated evidence of licensing-led institutional integration, while the U.S. remains more defined by issuer eligibility changes, capital treatment adjustments, and unresolved debates over stablecoin yield. At the same time, stablecoins are extending beyond simple payments into capital-markets settlement, payroll, treasury, embedded finance, and specialized asset-backed applications, but these expansions are occurring through clearly different operating models rather than a single market trajectory.

Signal: The strongest pattern in scope is the comparison between multiple institution-grade efforts to embed stablecoins directly into payment, treasury, and cross-border settlement infrastructure. The market is not moving through one universal model; instead, bank-led, card-led, and infrastructure-platform-led rails are developing in parallel under explicit compliance frameworks.

Evidence: Ripple’s approvals and platform expansion recur across the period, from FCA permissions in the UK and RLUSD-related regulatory progress to its end-to-end stablecoin platform and BC Payments acquisition in Australia. Strategic Implication: Stablecoin adoption is being anchored less by standalone token issuance than by integration into existing payment and settlement workflows. The comparative advantage is increasingly tied to regulated connectivity, fiat conversion, and operational interoperability rather than token branding alone.

Regulatory Pathways Diverge Sharply by Jurisdiction, with Europe and Parts of Asia More Licensing-Led Than the U.S.

Signal: A central comparison in the dataset is between jurisdictions that are formalizing stablecoin participation through licensing and supervised issuance pathways, and jurisdictions where policy remains shaped by eligibility debates, holding rules, and yield restrictions. Europe and several Asian markets show repeated movement toward structured entry routes, while the U.S. evidence is more mixed and politically contested.

Evidence: European evidence includes Crossmint’s MiCA authorization, Fipto’s CASP and Payment Institution licensing in France, DZ Bank’s MiCAR license tied to a euro stablecoin consortium, CHFAU’s MiCAR-compliant and BaFin-supervised launch, and OKX’s Malta payment institution license for compliant stablecoin products. Asian and adjacent jurisdictions show similar framework-building through Bank Negara Malaysia’s stablecoin and tokenized deposit sandbox, Hong Kong’s planned issuer licensing regime and later first licenses, HSBC’s stated interest in that regime, and Japan’s trust bank-backed JPYSC model. By contrast, U.S. developments center on recalibration rather than settled structure: the CFTC revised Staff Letter 25-40 to include national trust banks as issuers, the SEC reduced stablecoin haircuts to 2%, Florida advanced a state payment stablecoin bill, and OCC rulemaking under the GENIUS Act proposed standards for payment stablecoin issuers.

Strategic Implication: The comparative split suggests that stablecoin market access increasingly depends on jurisdiction-specific regulatory architecture. Where licensing pathways are clearer, institutional integration appears more directly linked to operational rollout; where policy remains contested, product design and competitive positioning remain more provisional.

Key Facts:
  • Europe shows repeated licensing-led integration through MiCA, MiCAR, CASP, and payment institution approvals.
  • Malaysia, Hong Kong, and Japan each appear with structured frameworks for supervised testing or issuance.
  • U.S. developments emphasize issuer eligibility, capital treatment, OCC rulemaking, and state-level legislation rather than a single settled operating model.
  • Hong Kong’s framework evolved from announced licensing plans to first licenses during the period.

Evidence: U.S. policy debate intensified through proposals to ban or limit passive yield, compromise discussions around “limited yield,” and repeated concern that stablecoin rewards could trigger deposit flight. Strategic Implication: Stablecoin competition is being shaped not only by settlement utility but also by whether yield is permitted, constrained, or structurally excluded. That distinction affects issuer economics, reserve strategy, and how closely stablecoins are allowed to compete with deposit-like products.
