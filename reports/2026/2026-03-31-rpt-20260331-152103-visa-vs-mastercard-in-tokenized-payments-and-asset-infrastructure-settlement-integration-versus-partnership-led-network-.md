## Executive Summary

Visa appears slightly ahead of Mastercard in the selected scope and the underlying evidence supports that lead through a broader, more operationally integrated stablecoin strategy. Across the period, Visa is repeatedly tied to settlement expansion, token issuance tooling, advisory services, multi-chain support, and payment-network integration, indicating a more end-to-end model built around embedding stablecoins into core money movement. Mastercard is also materially present, but its pattern is more concentrated around partnership-led network building, acquisitions, pilot programs, and ecosystem expansion through its Multi-Token Network and Crypto Partner Program. The comparison therefore centers less on whether both companies are active in tokenization, and more on how they differ: Visa leans toward direct payment and settlement enablement, while Mastercard leuses collaborations, infrastructure deals, and broader partner networks to connect digital assets with existing rails.

### Visa’s lead is supported by a broader end-to-end stablecoin settlement stack

In comparison with Mastercard, Visa is more consistently positioned around direct integration of stablecoins into payment and treasury workflows.

**Evidence:** Visa’s VTAP is described as enabling banks to issue digital tokens, including stablecoins and tokenized deposits, on public blockchains such as Ethereum. Visa also added a Global Stablecoins Advisory Practice, extending its role from infrastructure operator into implementation support for banks and fintechs.

**Strategic Implication:** This pattern suggests Visa’s edge in the selected scope comes from operational breadth rather than a single flagship initiative. The company is repeatedly linked to mechanisms that move stablecoins from experimentation toward embedded payment, settlement, and issuance workflows.

**Key Facts:**

### Mastercard’s core pattern is partnership-led infrastructure expansion rather than direct settlement breadth

**Signal:** Mastercard’s activity is structurally strong but more concentrated around building connective infrastructure through partnerships, acquisitions, and ecosystem programs. Compared with Visa’s more integrated payment-stack approach, Mastercard more often appears as an orchestrator of external capabilities and networked digital-asset access points.

**Evidence:** Mastercard’s Multi-Token Network was expanded to support tokenized fiat currencies, stablecoins, carbon credits, and treasury assets, while its MoonPay collaboration linked Mastercard-branded cards with crypto wallets for stablecoin-enabled wallet experiences. The company also pursued infrastructure expansion through acquisitions including Zerohash and BVNK, both described as strengthening stablecoin and tokenized-asset connectivity. Its partnership and pilot model extended into the Phase 2 e-HKD Pilot Programme with Airstar Bank and KBank, the ADI Foundation partnership focused on stablecoin settlement and tokenized assets, SoFiUSD integration on Mastercard’s network, and the Crypto Partner Program, which brought together more than 85 crypto-native companies and later included TRON. Mastercard also appeared in Solana’s enterprise platform context as part of a sandbox for stablecoin settlement and merchant applications.

**Strategic Implication:** Mastercard’s positioning suggests a network-expansion strategy built on assembling institutional and crypto-native counterparties around its rails. That gives it a broad collaboration footprint, but one that is more partner-mediated than Visa’s direct settlement and issuance integration.

**Key Facts:**

- Mastercard used acquisitions including Zerohash and BVNK to deepen crypto infrastructure and on-chain-to-fiat connectivity.

### The main contrast is operational integration at Visa versus ecosystem orchestration at Mastercard

**Signal:** The clearest comparative divide is not whether either company supports stablecoins, but how each translates tokenization into market structure. Visa’s pattern clusters around embedding stablecoins inside payment operations, while Mastercard’s pattern clusters around building a wider partner and infrastructure network around digital assets.

**Evidence:** Visa’s repeated examples point to integrated payment mechanisms: stablecoin settlement expansion, Visa Direct prefunding, continuous settlement operations, token issuance through VTAP, and card distribution with fintech partners. Mastercard’s repeated examples point to connective architecture: the Multi-Token Network, MoonPay wallet links, acquisitions of infrastructure providers, institutional pilots, and a large Crypto Partner Program. Even where both companies publicly downplayed stablecoins as an immediate threat to traditional card systems, the supporting evidence showed different emphases: Visa framed stablecoins as a complement to faster settlement and merchant or banking integration, while Mastercard emphasized existing network strengths such as reliability, distribution, and anti-fraud capabilities.

**Strategic Implication:** This distinction matters because it separates two different competitive routes into tokenized finance. Visa appears more focused on immediate workflow integration, while Mastercard appears more focused on scaling optionality through counterparties, platforms, and asset-network connectivity.

**Key Facts:**
- Visa’s evidence clusters around settlement, issuance, treasury, and card-linked deployment.
- Mastercard’s evidence clusters around network programs, acquisitions, pilots, and partner-led expansion.
- Both companies framed stablecoins as complementary to, rather than replacements for, traditional payment networks.
- Mastercard’s messaging highlighted reliability and anti-fraud, while Visa’s activity more often highlighted speed, settlement, and integration.
