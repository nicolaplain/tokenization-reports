## Executive Summary

Stablecoin development during the period is best understood as a comparison between two dominant structures: first, rapid buildout of regulated payment and settlement rails by banks, payment networks, and infrastructure providers; second, increasingly differentiated issuance and product models shaped by jurisdiction-specific regulation, collateral design, and yield constraints. Across the dataset, Europe and parts of Asia show repeated evidence of licensing-led institutional integration, while the U.S. remains more defined by issuer eligibility changes, capital treatment adjustments, and unresolved debates over stablecoin yield. At the same time, stablecoins are extending beyond simple payments into capital-markets settlement, payroll, treasury, embedded finance, and specialized asset-backed applications, but these expansions are occurring through clearly different operating models rather than a single market trajectory.

## Main Analytical Sections

### Regulated Payment and Settlement Rails Are the Dominant Stablecoin Development Path

**Signal:** The strongest pattern in scope is the comparison between multiple institution-grade efforts to embed stablecoins directly into payment, treasury, and cross-border settlement infrastructure. The market is not moving through one universal model; instead, bank-led, card-led, and infrastructure-platform-led rails are developing in parallel under explicit compliance frameworks.

**Evidence:** Ripple’s approvals and platform expansion recur across the period, from FCA permissions in the UK and RLUSD-related regulatory progress to its end-to-end stablecoin platform and BC Payments acquisition in Australia. Payment-network and enterprise integrations also broadened materially: Visa, Stripe, and Bridge expanded a stablecoin-backed card program to more than 100 countries; Mastercard enabled SoFiUSD for global settlement and later acquired BVNK; Triple-A joined Circle Payments Network for stablecoin-to-local-currency settlement. Bank- and infrastructure-linked examples reinforce the same mechanism through different designs, including ClearBank with Taurus, SGB Net’s regulated fiat-stablecoin interoperability service, Confirmo’s Paxos-powered U.S. enterprise payments, and SquareFi’s cross-border B2B stablecoin platform.

**Strategic Implication:** Stablecoin adoption is being anchored less by standalone token issuance than by integration into existing payment and settlement workflows. The comparative advantage is increasingly tied to regulated connectivity, fiat conversion, and operational interoperability rather than token branding alone.

**Key Facts:**
- Ripple secured FCA permissions in the UK, expanded an end-to-end stablecoin platform, and moved to acquire BC Payments in Australia.
- Visa, Stripe, and Bridge expanded a stablecoin-backed card program to over 100 countries.
- Mastercard both enabled SoFiUSD for settlement and acquired BVNK, indicating deeper network-level integration.
- SGB Net, Triple-A, Confirmo, and SquareFi each advanced stablecoin-enabled cross-border or enterprise payment infrastructure through distinct operating models.

### Regulatory Pathways Diverge Sharply by Jurisdiction, with Europe and Parts of Asia More Licensing-Led Than the U.S.

**Signal:** A central comparison in the dataset is between jurisdictions that are formalizing stablecoin participation through licensing and supervised issuance pathways, and jurisdictions where policy remains shaped by eligibility debates, holding rules, and yield restrictions. Europe and several Asian markets show repeated movement toward structured entry routes, while the U.S. evidence is more mixed and politically contested.

**Evidence:** European evidence includes Crossmint’s MiCA authorization, Fipto’s CASP and Payment Institution licensing in France, DZ Bank’s MiCAR license tied to a euro stablecoin consortium, CHFAU’s MiCAR-compliant and BaFin-supervised launch, and OKX’s Malta payment institution license for compliant stablecoin products. Asian and adjacent jurisdictions show similar framework-building through Bank Negara Malaysia’s stablecoin and tokenized deposit sandbox, Hong Kong’s planned issuer licensing regime and later first licenses, HSBC’s stated interest in that regime, and Japan’s trust bank-backed JPYSC model. By contrast, U.S. developments center on recalibration rather than settled structure: the CFTC revised Staff Letter 25-40 to include national trust banks as issuers, the SEC reduced stablecoin haircuts to 2%, Florida advanced a state payment stablecoin bill, and OCC rulemaking under the GENIUS Act proposed standards for payment stablecoin issuers.

**Strategic Implication:** The comparative split suggests that stablecoin market access increasingly depends on jurisdiction-specific regulatory architecture. Where licensing pathways are clearer, institutional integration appears more directly linked to operational rollout; where policy remains contested, product design and competitive positioning remain more provisional.

**Key Facts:**
- Europe shows repeated licensing-led integration through MiCA, MiCAR, CASP, and payment institution approvals.
- Malaysia, Hong Kong, and Japan each appear with structured frameworks for supervised testing or issuance.
- U.S. developments emphasize issuer eligibility, capital treatment, OCC rulemaking, and state-level legislation rather than a single settled operating model.
- Hong Kong’s framework evolved from announced licensing plans to first licenses during the period.

### Yield Rules and Product Economics Are a Major Structural Fault Line, Especially in the U.S.

**Signal:** One of the clearest comparison points is between stablecoins positioned as pure payment instruments and stablecoins or platforms designed around yield, lending, or treasury income. The strongest tension appears where policymakers and banking groups push to limit yield-like features while market participants continue building yield-bearing products.

**Evidence:** U.S. policy debate intensified through proposals to ban or limit passive yield, compromise discussions around “limited yield,” and repeated concern that stablecoin rewards could trigger deposit flight. These debates were reflected in market sensitivity, including reporting that Circle’s valuation fell sharply on possible yield restrictions. On the product side, multiple models continued to expand: Sky Frontier’s USDS was framed as a yield-generating stablecoin with supply growth and reserve-focused adjustments; Fosun Wealth’s FUSD linked stablecoin design to money market funds and government bonds; Lido launched EarnUSD; Theo introduced thUSD linked to tokenized gold; and institutional treasury or yield platforms emerged through Black Titan Corp, Zircuit Finance, Bybit EU, and Japan’s SBI VC Trade retail USDC lending service.

**Strategic Implication:** Stablecoin competition is being shaped not only by settlement utility but also by whether yield is permitted, constrained, or structurally excluded. That distinction affects issuer economics, reserve strategy, and how closely stablecoins are allowed to compete with deposit-like products.

**Key Facts:**
- U.S. debate over stablecoin yield moved from broad restriction proposals toward reported compromise discussions on limited yield.
- Market reaction to potential restrictions was significant enough to affect reported stablecoin-linked valuations.
- Yield-oriented stablecoin models ranged from protocol-native structures to RWA-backed and commodity-linked designs.
- Treasury and lending products are expanding even as regulatory treatment of yield remains unsettled.

### Stablecoin Issuance Models Are Fragmenting Across Fiat, Commodity-Backed, Sovereign, and Institutional Custody Structures

**Signal:** A major comparative pattern is the fragmentation of issuance design rather than convergence on a single reserve model. Stablecoins in scope differ materially by backing logic, issuer structure, and target market, including bank-issued, trust-bank-backed, commodity-linked, state-linked, and institution-custodied models.

**Evidence:** Institution-oriented fiat models include USDU in the UAE with onshore bank-held reserves, CHFAU for Swiss franc-denominated institutional settlement, JPYSC as Japan’s first trust bank-backed yen stablecoin, and FYUSD with BitGo named as issuer and custodian for an Asia-focused launch. Other issuer structures point in different directions: Payoneer sought a national trust bank charter for PAYO-USD; Wyoming introduced a state-backed digital currency initiative; and Wells Fargo filed a trademark for WFUSD while TruStage pursued a stablecoin for U.S. credit unions. Alternative collateral models also remained visible, including OIL1’s oil-backed design, Theo’s gold-linked thUSD, Mega Matrix and Ihsan Dhahab’s Shariah-compliant precious-metals ecosystem, and Datavault AI with American Strategic Minerals targeting a critical-minerals-backed stablecoin model.

**Strategic Implication:** The issuance landscape is not standardizing around one reserve template. Instead, regulation, target customer base, and collateral philosophy are producing specialized issuance forms, which may widen adoption pathways but also increase market segmentation.

**Key Facts:**
- Multiple bank- and institution-linked fiat issuance models appeared across the UAE, Europe, Japan, and Asia-focused launches.
- U.S.-linked issuer strategies included charter-seeking, trademark filings, and credit-union-specific designs.
- Commodity- and metals-linked structures remained active alongside fiat-pegged models.
- State- or sovereign-linked approaches also appeared, but as a distinct subset rather than the dominant path.

### Capital-Markets and Securities Settlement Use Cases Show a Different Integration Logic Than Everyday Payments

**Signal:** Stablecoin adoption in capital markets is developing through a distinct mechanism from retail and commerce payments: atomic settlement, 24/7 trading access, and securities liquidity workflows. This creates a clear comparison between consumer-facing payment expansion and institution-facing market-infrastructure modernization.

**Evidence:** Nomura and Daiwa, with major banks, piloted a yen-pegged stablecoin for atomic securities settlement through the “3 Mega SC” proof-of-concept. Société Générale extended EURCV onto XRP Ledger for institutional-grade settlement, while OTCM Protocol used the GENIUS Act framework to support liquidity for abandoned OTC securities through regulated stablecoin settlement. Additional market-structure examples include BitMEX’s equity perpetuals settled in stablecoins, StableStock’s platform for stablecoin access to Hong Kong-listed equities, and Ripple’s reported participation in a Singapore trade settlement pilot through RLUSD.

**Strategic Implication:** Stablecoins are becoming embedded in market plumbing, not just payment interfaces. The comparison with everyday payments shows a second adoption track in which the main value lies in synchronized settlement and liquidity access rather than merchant acceptance.

**Key Facts:**
- Nomura and Daiwa tested yen-pegged stablecoin settlement for bonds and equities.
- Société Générale used EURCV on XRP Ledger for faster institutional settlement.
- OTCM Protocol and Ripple-linked pilots point to stablecoin use in specialized securities and trade-settlement workflows.
- BitMEX and StableStock show trading-product models that use stablecoins to extend access and operating hours.

### Everyday Commerce, Payroll, and Embedded Finance Are Expanding Through Multiple Distribution Models

**Signal:** Compared with capital-markets settlement, consumer and workforce adoption is advancing through a broader mix of cards, wallets, payroll systems, neobanks, QR-based merchant tools, and embedded accounts. The key comparison is between direct-to-consumer utility models and business-integrated payout and wallet models.

**Evidence:** Retail and card-linked examples include OrbitX Pay on XDC for USDC spending at more than 150 million merchants, the Rumble Wallet launch with Tether, Rizon’s non-custodial stablecoin neobank, and Maya’s QR-based merchant payment model with same-day settlement. Workforce and embedded-finance models include Gusto’s global stablecoin payout pilot, Ontop’s embedded USD stablecoin accounts for global workers, Bitget Wallet’s bank-transfer payout feature in Bangladesh, and Meta’s exploration of a third-party-supported stablecoin and wallet integration for social commerce. Tether’s investment in Whop and WLFI’s World Swap expansion further show marketplace and remittance-oriented distribution strategies rather than bank-led rollout.

**Strategic Implication:** Stablecoin utility is broadening through multiple access layers rather than one dominant front-end. Distribution strategy now matters as much as reserve design, with different platforms targeting merchants, creators, workers, remittance users, and online marketplace participants.

**Key Facts:**
- Consumer-facing models span crypto cards, self-custodial wallets, neobanks, and QR-code merchant acceptance.
- Payroll and workforce models are emerging through Gusto, Ontop, and local payout integrations.
- Social-commerce and marketplace integrations are being explored through Meta, Whop, and Rumble-linked models.
- These approaches differ from bank-led infrastructure by focusing on end-user distribution and embedded access.

### Specialized and Experimental Stablecoin Deployments Remain Active but More Fragmented

**Signal:** Outside the dominant payment-rail and licensing narratives, the dataset shows a fragmented layer of specialized stablecoin experimentation. These initiatives are structurally important as contrasts, but they are narrower in scope and less repeated than regulated payment integration.

**Evidence:** Examples include stablecoin-linked healthcare reimbursement through Solum Global and DFINITY’s sgUSD, a critical-minerals-backed tokenization model from Datavault AI and American Strategic Minerals, and AI-agent payment infrastructure from Tempo and World Liberty Financial’s USD1 plans. Additional specialized cases include DRB-Hicom’s ringgit-backed intra-group exploration, Trump’s “Board of Peace” exploration of a USD stablecoin for Gaza, and kpk’s zero-loss emergency response after the Resolv USR stablecoin collapse. These are joined by niche community or mission-oriented models such as Catholic USD and by infrastructure designs such as PNXP’s Crypto2Cash Loans platform.

**Strategic Implication:** The outer edge of the market remains highly experimental, with stablecoins being adapted to narrow operational environments and sector-specific problems. These models expand the design space, but they do not yet display the same concentration or institutional standardization as regulated payment and settlement rails.

**Key Facts:**
- Specialized deployments span healthcare, critical minerals, AI-agent payments, crisis-region use, intra-group settlement, and community-focused models.
- The Resolv collapse and kpk response highlight that operational risk management remains a live issue for some onchain stablecoin structures.
- These developments are more heterogeneous than the bank-, network-, and licensing-led payment infrastructure trend.
- Several specialized models combine stablecoins with broader tokenization or automation initiatives rather than pure payments.
