## Executive Summary

Hong Kong leads the selected scope through a more concentrated pattern of regulatory-led tokenization across funds and debt, with repeated evidence of government frameworks, SFC approvals, licensed distribution, and institutional issuance models supporting product expansion. Singapore appears as a material but more diversified secondary market, with stronger evidence of platform partnerships, white-label infrastructure, regulated access models, and multi-asset experimentation spanning funds, debt, equity, real estate, and natural resources. Across the comparison, the clearest structural contrast is that Hong Kong repeatedly anchors tokenization through formal market structure and regulated issuance pathways, while Singapore more often appears as the execution, distribution, and product-enablement environment; cross-border collaborations increasingly connect these two roles.

## Main Analytical Sections

### Hong Kong’s lead rests on regulatory-led conversion of funds and debt into institutionally structured tokenized products

**Signal:** Hong Kong’s strongest structural advantage in the dataset is the repeated use of regulatory and licensed-market mechanisms to move tokenized funds and debt products from pilot activity toward institutionally structured issuance and distribution. This pattern aligns with Hong Kong’s leading country position in the selected scope and is most visible in the two largest in-scope asset classes, funds and debt.

**Evidence:** For funds, Hong Kong appears repeatedly in tokenized money market and fund workflows: China Financial Leasing Group raised HK$86.7 million for a tokenized digital money market fund; China Merchants Bank’s Hong Kong arm tokenized a USD money market fund on BNB Chain; Bosera and HashKey were reported to launch tokenized money market ETFs; and Chainlink and UBS demonstrated a tokenized fund workflow through DigiFT that automated subscription and redemption. Hong Kong’s policy layer reinforced this activity through SFC-backed digital asset fund oversight, support measures for bond tokenization expenses, and broader policy messaging around tokenized asset frameworks and preferential regulations for funds. On the debt side, the HKSAR Government’s digitally native green notes and Hong Kong’s third blockchain-based green bond, including settlement via tokenized central bank money, sit alongside private-sector structures such as Towngas’s HK$100 million credit-facility-backed project, QNB’s digital bond listed in Hong Kong, and later debt-oriented issuance plans by NIVF and NewGen. By early 2026, Hong Kong’s regulated product set had expanded further through Hang Seng Gold ETF tokenized non-listed fund units, Deyling Holdings’ SFC-approved RWA products, and EX.IO’s broader regulated fixed-income matrix.

**Strategic Implication:** The evidence points to Hong Kong building tokenization through formal issuance channels, supervisory support, and licensed infrastructure rather than through isolated product launches alone. That makes Hong Kong’s position structurally stronger where institutional credibility, settlement design, and regulatory compatibility matter most.

**Key Facts:**
- Hong Kong fund tokenization evidence includes money market funds, money market ETFs, tokenized fund workflows, and tokenized non-listed fund units.
- Hong Kong debt tokenization evidence spans government green notes, digital green bonds, private credit structures, and regulated fixed-income product expansion.
- Regulatory support includes the Digital Bond Grant Scheme, SFC-backed oversight initiatives, SFC approval activity, and broader government policy signaling.

### Singapore’s comparative strength is broader multi-asset platformization rather than a single dominant issuance pathway

**Signal:** Singapore’s tokenization profile is less concentrated than Hong Kong’s and more consistently expressed through platform partnerships, white-label enablement, regulated access rails, and multi-asset product design. Rather than one dominant mechanism, Singapore shows a broader execution-layer role across funds, debt, equity, real estate, and natural resources.

**Evidence:** In funds, Singapore-linked activity includes Chintai and Passion Venture Capital’s white-label tokenization collaboration, Maybank AM Singapore’s tokenized fund, CapBridge’s access model for tokenized money market funds with Franklin Templeton, TradeTogether’s full CMS licence for tokenized asset fund management, and FundBridge’s role in tokenized fund structures including Wellington Management’s treasury fund. In debt, Singapore appears through Pendle’s fixed-yield activity, MAS trials of tokenized bills and bonds settled in CBDC-related frameworks, ADDX’s alternative financing partnership with GB Helios, and FundBridge Capital’s tokenized gold-linked private credit fund. In equity and broader RWA enablement, the market includes StableStock and Native’s 24/7 tokenized U.S. equity trading on BNB Chain, 1exchange and Capitaux’s ISIN-backed NVDAt, Ark International’s digital equity token program, ADDX’s white-label institutional platform, and Edena-related real-asset initiatives tied to real estate and carbon credits. Singapore’s structured regulatory layer also appears explicitly in MAS’s revised guide on tokenisation of capital markets products.

**Strategic Implication:** Singapore’s role in the dataset is that of a flexible, product-enablement and distribution hub able to support multiple asset classes through compliant infrastructure. Compared with Hong Kong’s tighter concentration around formal regulated issuance, Singapore appears structurally broader but less centered on one dominant tokenization pathway.

**Key Facts:**
- Singapore evidence spans funds, debt, equity, real estate, and natural-resources-linked structures rather than one primary asset-class channel.
- White-label and regulated access models recur through Chintai, CapBridge, FundBridge, ADDX, TradeTogether, and 1exchange.
- MAS guidance provides an explicit regulatory frame across tokenized debt, equity, and fund structures.

### Funds are the clearest comparison point, but Hong Kong and Singapore specialize differently within the same asset class

**Signal:** Funds are the leading asset class in the selected scope, yet the two markets do not approach fund tokenization in the same way. Hong Kong’s fund activity is more closely tied to regulated fund conversion and institutional fund operations, while Singapore’s fund activity more often centers on access infrastructure, white-label distribution, and interoperable packaging.

**Evidence:** Hong Kong’s fund pattern includes tokenized digital money market funds, tokenized money market ETFs, automated onchain subscription and redemption workflows, and SFC-linked oversight and framework support. Examples include China Financial Leasing Group’s tokenized money market fund raise, China Merchants Bank Hong Kong’s tokenized USD money market fund, Bosera and HashKey’s tokenized money market ETFs, Feng Innovation’s SFC-framed tokenized fund-of-funds, and Hang Seng Gold ETF tokenized non-listed fund units. Singapore’s fund pattern includes Chintai’s white-label collaboration, CapBridge’s regulated access to tokenized money market funds, Maybank AM Singapore’s tokenized fund, FundBridge’s facilitation layer in treasury-fund structures, and TradeTogether’s licence-backed fund management pathway. Even where the two ecosystems intersect, as in Wellington Management’s treasury fund facilitated by FundBridge, the comparison still reinforces Hong Kong’s regulated product framing versus Singapore’s platform and distribution role.

**Strategic Implication:** The fund segment does not show a simple winner-takes-all pattern; instead it reveals distinct specialization. Hong Kong appears stronger in regulated fund structuring and supervisory integration, while Singapore appears stronger in packaging, access, and platform-led deployment models.

**Key Facts:**
- Hong Kong fund tokenization repeatedly centers on money market funds, ETFs, and regulated operational workflows.
- Singapore fund tokenization repeatedly centers on white-label access, regulated distribution, and platform-mediated investor entry.
- Cross-border fund collaborations connect Hong Kong-regulated product framing with Singapore-based execution infrastructure.

### Debt tokenization shows the sharpest maturity contrast: Hong Kong issuance scale versus Singapore pilot and yield-market experimentation

**Signal:** Debt is nearly as prominent as funds in the selected scope, and it is the asset class where the contrast between Hong Kong and Singapore is most structurally explicit. Hong Kong shows repeat issuance, listing, and regulated fixed-income expansion, while Singapore shows a mix of pilot issuance, digital yield experimentation, and alternative financing structures.

**Evidence:** Hong Kong’s debt evidence includes its third digital green bond with tokenized central bank money settlement, the HKSAR Government’s multi-currency digitally native notes, QNB’s Hong Kong-listed native digital bond via HSBC Orion, Towngas’s credit-facility-backed RWA project, later tokenized bond plans by NIVF, and NewGen’s tokenized real estate bond campaign. This pattern is reinforced by HKEX’s fixed income and currency roadmap and EX.IO’s regulated debt products such as TBILL and short-term liquidity notes. Singapore’s debt evidence is materially present but structurally different: MAS trial issuance of tokenized bills and bonds points to a policy-led testing phase, Pendle’s fixed-yield activity reflects a digital yield-market route rather than public-market issuance, ADDX and GB Helios focus on alternative financing, and FundBridge’s gold-linked private credit fund combines debt exposure with natural-resources linkage. The comparison repeatedly shows Hong Kong in scaled, regulated issuance structures and Singapore in experimentation and product innovation.

**Strategic Implication:** The debt segment suggests Hong Kong is further along in institutional issuance mechanics, while Singapore is developing adjacent debt-tokenization capabilities through pilots, programmable yield markets, and financing platforms. For market structure, that creates a maturity gap without eliminating Singapore’s importance as a secondary debt innovation center.

**Key Facts:**
- Hong Kong debt tokenization includes sovereign-style issuance, exchange-linked digital bonds, private credit tokenization, and regulated fixed-income product expansion.
- Singapore debt tokenization includes MAS-led tokenized bill and bond trials, fixed-yield digital markets, alternative financing, and tokenized private credit structures.
- The contrast is less about debt presence versus absence and more about issuance maturity versus experimentation.

### Equity tokenization reveals different market-access models: Hong Kong links tokens to listed-market structure, Singapore emphasizes continuous and standardized digital access

**Signal:** Equity tokenization is a secondary but material segment where Hong Kong and Singapore again diverge in mechanism. Hong Kong-linked examples more often connect tokenized equity access to listed-market or licensed-broker structures, while Singapore-linked examples stress continuous trading and standardized security-token form.

**Evidence:** On the Hong Kong side, Starcoin Foundation’s initiative tied its token to HKEX-listed stock positions, and StableStock later offered trading access to more than 160 Hong Kong-listed securities using stablecoins through a partnership with licensed broker HabitTrade during Hong Kong trading hours. Hong Kong’s broader compliant securities infrastructure was also reinforced through Metafyed and NXMarket as well as EX.IO’s regulated tokenized securities expansion. In Singapore, StableStock and Native launched 24/7 tokenized U.S. equity trading on BNB Chain using a 1:1 sToken model, Ark International launched a digital equity token program in Singapore, and 1exchange with Capitaux introduced NVDAt as an ISIN-backed 1:1 NVIDIA-linked tokenized security. Together these examples show Singapore favoring persistent digital availability and standardization, versus Hong Kong’s closer attachment to existing listed-market and broker-mediated structures.

**Strategic Implication:** The equity comparison shows that both jurisdictions support tokenized stock exposure, but through different market design assumptions. Hong Kong leans toward regulated bridge models into existing market structure, while Singapore shows more emphasis on extending trading format and token standardization.

**Key Facts:**
- Hong Kong equity tokenization examples include HKEX-linked token-stock structures and stablecoin access to Hong Kong-listed shares via a licensed broker.
- Singapore equity tokenization examples include 24/7 tokenized U.S. equity trading and ISIN-backed tokenized securities.
- The contrast is structural: listed-market integration in Hong Kong versus extended-access and standardized token format in Singapore.

### Natural resources and real estate remain secondary segments, but they show clearer asymmetry than funds and debt

**Signal:** Natural resources and real estate are smaller in-scope asset classes, yet they reveal a sharper asymmetry between the two markets. Hong Kong’s natural-resources activity is more visible and policy-connected, while Singapore shows more experimentation across environmental and precious-metals-linked structures; real estate is present in both markets but with more fragmented pathways and mixed regulatory treatment.

**Evidence:** In natural resources, Hong Kong examples include Esperanza’s tokenized gold initiative, OSL Group and Antalpha’s work around Tether Gold, and Finloop’s gold-mining-asset-backed notes launched with Midas Gold Resources and AnchorV alongside Hong Kong policy incentives to attract gold refiners and promote blockchain-tokenized gold. Singapore’s natural-resources activity includes the national plastics passport and Plastic Cycle Token model, Mega Matrix’s collaboration on an Islamic precious-metals RWA ecosystem, and FundBridge’s gold-linked private credit fund. In real estate, Hong Kong showed regulatory caution around LABS Security Token but later moved toward a first SFC-approved real-estate RWA case through Deyling Holdings, while K-Prop used Hong Kong as the global Web3 expansion hub in a Hong Kong–Seoul structure. Singapore-related real-estate evidence is more platform-led, including Edena’s STO-based real-asset approach, Chintai’s multi-asset coverage including real estate, Redacted and Tenset’s multi-vertical strategy, and ADDX’s white-label support for private-market assets including real estate.

**Strategic Implication:** These smaller asset classes suggest that Hong Kong is extending its regulated-market playbook into commodities and selected real estate structures, while Singapore is using broader platform experimentation to test adjacent asset types. The asymmetry is stronger here because these segments are less standardized and therefore more dependent on either regulatory sponsorship or flexible platform design.

**Key Facts:**
- Hong Kong natural-resources evidence is concentrated around tokenized gold and gold-linked infrastructure, with accompanying policy support.
- Singapore natural-resources evidence spans recycled-plastics tokenization, precious-metals ecosystem building, and gold-linked credit structures.
- Real-estate tokenization remains more fragmented than funds or debt, with both opportunity and regulatory friction visible across the two markets.

### Cross-border pairings increasingly combine Hong Kong’s regulatory weight with Singapore’s platform and distribution capabilities

**Signal:** A recurring relationship pattern across the summaries is the formation of cross-border tokenization pairings that link Hong Kong and Singapore rather than treating them as isolated ecosystems. These collaborations repeatedly combine Hong Kong-linked compliance or regulated issuance credibility with Singapore-linked trading, platform, or distribution infrastructure.
